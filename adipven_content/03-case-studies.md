### Malaysia: YKL Engineering Defeated During Appeal

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-ykl-engineering-defeated-during-appeal/
**Date:** Not shown

YKL Engineering Sdn. Bhd. sued Sungai Kahang Palm Oil Sdn. Bhd. and Profina Teknik Sdn. Bhd. for patent and copyright infringement regarding a "Fruit Bunch Splitter" machine (Patent No. MY-139512-A). The High Court ruled for YKL Engineering, but the Court of Appeal reversed this decision, finding in favor of the defendants.

**Patent Construction:** The appellate court found that the High Court failed to properly construe the patent itself, improperly delegating this duty to expert witnesses. Courts must apply "a purposive approach" when interpreting patents.

**Prior Art Evidence:** The court clarified that while anticipation defenses must be pleaded, defendants may introduce additional prior art examples during trial without prejudice if pleadings were adequate.

**Expert Evidence:** The court rejected the lower court's dismissal of expert reports merely because they shared similar phrasing, holding that "similarities in phrases and words used...can be regarded as a norm or standard."

**Confidentiality Claims:** The court determined that information cannot escape prior art status simply through confidentiality, applying the *Coco v A.N. Clark* test requiring information quality, confidential circumstances, and unauthorized use.

**Copyright:** The court held that the plaintiff's drawings were substantially reproductions of prior art, preventing copyright protection. Without copyright ownership, infringement claims failed.

### Malaysia: Patent – Ultimate Decision on Dependent Claim's Survival in Court

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-patent-ultimate-decision-on-dependent-claims-survival-in-court/
**Date:** Not shown

This landmark Federal Court decision addressed whether dependent patent claims automatically become invalid when their independent claim is invalidated.

**Parties:** Merck Sharp & Dohme Group and Merck Sharp & Dohme (Malaysia) Sdn Bhd (Appellants) v. Hovid Berhad (Respondent)

**Patent at Issue:** Malaysian patent MY-118194-A for alendronate (Fosamax), containing one independent claim and 21 dependent claims.

**Lower Court Outcomes:** The High Court dismissed the infringement action on August 30, 2016, finding the independent claim obvious. The Court of Appeal upheld this decision on September 19, 2017, ruling all dependent claims automatically invalid.

**Federal Court Leave Granted:** June 26, 2018, on the single question of whether dependent claims necessarily fail when their independent claim is invalidated.

**Key Holding:** The majority judgment reversed precedent, holding that "dependent claims may not necessarily automatically fail" when an independent claim is invalid. The court emphasized the importance of examining each dependent claim separately, considering whether they contain additional features beyond the independent claim. The case was remitted to the High Court for individual validity determinations.

### Malaysia: How Patent Damages Are Accessed by Court

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-how-patent-damages-are-accessed-by-court/
**Date:** Not shown

This article discusses the Court of Appeal decision in *Asia File Products Sdn Bhd v Brilliant Achievement Sdn Bhd, Lion File Marketing Sdn and Kho Kok Seang*, concerning compensatory damages assessment for patent infringement in Malaysia.

**The Patent:** Malaysian Patent No. MY-137755-A covering a tool-free box file with a clip mechanism for holding documents.

**The Parties:** Plaintiff — Asia File Products Sdn Bhd (patent owner). Defendants — Brilliant Achievement Sdn Bhd (manufacturer), Lion File Marketing Sdn (distributor), and Kho Kok Seang (director).

**Key Issues Addressed:** (1) Admissibility of witness statements without corresponding affidavits; (2) Application of royalty basis versus lost profit basis for damages calculation; (3) Entitlement to lost profits across all infringing products.

**Court Findings:** The Court determined that "the Plaintiff was entitled to claim for loss of profit arising from all the infringing products because it is probable that a reasonable customer of a box file would have purchased an infringing product thinking that such a product is a patented product."

**Outcome:** Reassessed damages: MYR1,558,472.45. Interest: 5% per annum from March 30, 2017 until full payment. No costs awarded for prior assessment proceedings.

### Malaysia: Kingtime is Victorious

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-kingtime-is-victorious/
**Date:** No date shown on the page.

In November 2018, Malaysia's High Court of Malaya in Kuala Lumpur ruled on patent infringement principles in *Kingtime International Limited and Gryphon Energy (Asia-Pacific) Sdn. Bhd v Petrofac E&C Sdn. Bhd*.

Kingtime held two patents (MY-144898-A and MY-145004-A) covering "Mobile Offshore Production Unit" (MOPU) technology. Gryphon Energy held a license to these patents. Both plaintiffs alleged Petrofac infringed the patents through design, manufacture, and operation of a MOPU in Sepat oil field off Terengganu's coast.

The core dispute centered on interpreting "removably attached" in the patent claims. Plaintiffs argued this meant structures designed for removal during their operational life. Defendants contended it referred only to attachment methods and cited welding as prior art, arguing the patents lacked novelty.

The court applied three infringement tests: Essential Integers, Improver's test, and Actavis' test. All three supported finding infringement. The court accepted the plaintiffs' expert interpretation that the wellhead deck and subsea conductor frame were modular building blocks capable of independent use.

**Outcome:** The court found Petrofac infringed all three claims through its construction, installation, and operation of the Sepat MOPU.

### Malaysia: Patent Found to Invalid

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-patent-found-to-invalid/
**Date:** Not shown

In a patent infringement dispute between Hong Yik Plastics (M) Sdn Bhd. ("the Plaintiff") and Ho Shen Lee (M) Sdn Bhd. and TNL Plastic Manufacturer Sdn Bhd. ("the Defendants"), the High Court addressed two key issues: whether patent infringement occurred, and whether the Plaintiff's patent was invalid.

**Limitation Period Issue:** Section 59(3) of the Patents Act 1953 restricts patent infringement proceedings to within 5 years of the infringing act. The Plaintiff failed to plead the infringement date in their Statement of Claim. Based on evidence, the Court determined infringement occurred before February 10, 2012, but the suit was filed March 13, 2017 — exceeding the statutory period. Consequently, the claim was dismissed.

**Patent Validity Issue:** The Defendants counterclaimed for patent invalidation. The Court found the Plaintiff's inventor "relied heavily on his patent agent" for the application process. Critically, the patent agent failed to disclose existing prior art in the patent documentation.

The Court invalidated the patent, determining the invention lacked novelty due to existing prior art and incomplete disclosure to the Patent Registrar. The Defendant's counterclaim succeeded with costs awarded.

### Cambodia: European Patents Can Now Be Validated in Cambodia

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/cambodia-european-patents-can-now-be-validated-in-cambodia/
**Date:** No date shown

The President of the European Patent Office (EPO) and the Cambodian Minister of Industry and Handcraft signed an agreement on the validation of European patents that came into effect since 1 March 2018. This sets Cambodia to become the first country in Asia to validate European patents in the Asian region and now part of the 38-member states of the EPO. From the date of 1 March 2018, it will be possible to validate European patents applications in Cambodia; after validation, the patents will confer the same protections as patents granted by the EPO. This agreement aids in the reduction of patent processing time, costs incurred to applicants, and administrative burden faced by the Cambodian Patent Office.

The following requirements must be met in order to validate European patents in Cambodia:

1. The European patent must have a filing date that falls on or after 1 March 2018. Validation is not available for patents filed prior to 1 March 2018;
2. The European patent must align with the Law on Patents in force in Cambodia, an example of which pharmaceutical products are excluded from patent protection until the Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of IP Rights (TRIPS) have expired; and
3. The claims of the European patents must be translated into the Khmer Language.

A validation fee of EUR180 must be paid to the EPO within six (6) months from publication of the European Search Report provided by the EPO. After the expiry of the above basic time limit, the validation fee can still be paid within a two months grace period in which a surcharge of 50% is paid on top of the validation fee. If the validation fee is not paid within the period, the request for validation will be deemed withdrawn. Within three months of the grant by EPO, the applicant will then need to submit a request to validate the European patent as a Cambodian national patent with the Ministry of Industry and Handicraft.

### India: Requirements of Working Statements

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/india-requirements-of-working-statements/
**Date:** Not shown

This article relates to a recent order by the High Court of Delhi relating to submission of working statements of inventions which have been patented in India.

Under the Indian patent laws, every owner of a granted patent is required to submit a statement regarding working of the patented invention on a commercial scale in India. The working statements are required to be submitted annually in a prescribed form (Form 27) with a deadline of 31 March every year for the preceding calendar year. Failure to file a working statement with complete and correct information may entail imposition of fine as well as initiation of criminal proceedings and punishment of imprisonment up to 6 months.

In the decision of the Court, reference was made to a way adopted by patentees to circumvent this requirement by stating that details of licenses are confidential in nature and such details shall be provided under specific directions from the Patent Office. The Court held that patentees are required to submit the details of licenses and sub-licenses granted by the patentee and such information cannot be considered confidential. The Court further held that any suppression of information relating to the details of licenses and sub-licenses should be treated as failure to comply with the requirements of the Indian Patents Act and directed the Indian Patent Office to take action against patentees who do not furnish the required information.

It must also be noted that the Court held that the argument that Form 27 being extremely vague in nature should not be an excuse for non-compliance with a statutory requirement.

Although the order is not a final decision as an appeal may be filed against this decision to the Indian Supreme Court, it is mandatory for patentees to file the working statements of their patented invention to the Indian Patent Office before the prescribed deadline.

### Malaysia: Court of Appeal Held Skyworld Mark is Well-Known

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-court-of-appeal-held-skyworld-mark-is-well-known/
**Date:** No publication date shown.

*Skyworld Development Sdn Bhd & Anor v Skyworld Holdings Sdn Bhd & Ors* involves trademark infringement, passing off and unlawful interference with trade.

The Plaintiffs are affiliated private limited companies in real estate and property development, holding registered ownership of "SkyWorld" in Malaysia since 2014. The Defendants operate tourism businesses using "Skyworld", "Sky World", and "Sky World City Sabah" in company names, domain names, and a Sabah development project.

Plaintiffs sued for trademark infringement, claiming the infringing marks closely resembled their registered trademark and would likely cause consumer confusion. The High Court dismissed the claim, finding the infringing marks visually different and any similarities coincidental. The court determined Plaintiffs failed proving trademark infringement requirements under Section 38 of the Trade Marks Act 1976 and couldn't demonstrate sufficient goodwill for passing off.

Dissatisfied, Plaintiffs appealed to the Court of Appeal (CoA), contending the judge erred in law and fact. The CoA unanimously allowed the appeal, setting aside the High Court's decision in Plaintiffs' favor.

**Trade Mark Infringement Elements:** Under Section 38 of the Trade Marks Act 1976, parties must prove ownership of a valid registered trademark, unauthorized use by the infringer during business operations, and that use caused deception or confusion. The burden falls on Plaintiffs. Section 3(2) defines "use" as "printed or other visual representation" of marks; for services, use means "statement or part of statement about availability or performance of services."

**Trademark vs. Business/Company/Domain Names:** A trademark identifies products and distinguishes them from competitors; registered trademarks receive protection under statute and general law, unregistered marks receive general law protection only. A business name identifies a business — if the owner's name differs from the business name, registration is required; a trademark matching a business name may register but serves different purposes. A company name refers to the legal entity owning a business; trademarks matching company names are registrable. A domain name is an internet address enabling website access; domains commonly match trademarks, business, or company names but are registered solely for internet addressing.

**Similarity Analysis:** The High Court found no significant similarities between the marks beyond word similarity, noting the registered mark featured uppercase/lowercase variation and an underline stroke; therefore, no resemblance existed. The CoA disagreed, citing *Low Chi Yong (t/a Reynox Fertichem Industries) v Low Chi Hong & Anor* [2018] 1 MLJ 175, holding that infringement requires "unauthorized use of a sign that is substantially identical with, or deceptively similar to, a registered trademark." The court need not examine minute differences; relevance concerns whether the infringing mark "so nearly resembles the registered trademark, to cause confusion or deceive prospective customers."

**Company Name Registration Significance:** Defendants conceded that Registrar of Companies registration validates names and shouldn't be challenged. Plaintiffs countered that the Registrar should have rejected applications under Section 26 of the Companies Act 2016, as names were identical to Plaintiffs' names, and argued CoA registration doesn't prevent trademark infringement actions. The CoA disagreed, finding Defendants' names not identical to Plaintiffs' names, thus no Section 26(2) Companies Act 2016 contravention. The Registrar's Section 26(2) powers don't include refusing registration when names offend existing trademarks. However, "registering of a business name does not preclude a person from bringing an action for trademark infringement." The CoA referenced an Australian article from Murfett Legal stating "registration of business name, company name or a website domain does not confer some form of ownership or exclusive rights to the use of the name."

**Trademark Use Relative to Goods vs. Company Names:** Defendants submitted that Sections 30 and 3(2) of the Trade Marks Act 1976 require identical/similar mark usage "in relation to goods or services" — merely using similar marks proves insufficient, citing European Court of Justice cases. The CoA cited *Celine SARL v Celine SA* (2007) C-17/06: "the purpose of a company, trade or shop name is not, of itself, to distinguish goods or services where, it is limited to identifying a company or designating a business which is being carried on, such use cannot be considered as being 'in relation to goods or services'." However, "if the sign is also affixed to goods which are marketed or is used in such a way that a link is established between the sign and the goods or services, there is use 'in relation to goods or services'." The European Court applied a two-stage test: first, determine if company/trade/shop name use affects or might affect trademark functions; second, assess whether third-party use follows honest practices in industrial/commercial matters. Applying this test, the CoA rejected the High Court's finding that Defendants' mark use was coincidental, finding Defendants weren't acting honestly and were "tortfeasors attempting to ride on the Plaintiffs' goodwill."

**Confusion/Deception:** Establishing trademark infringement requires showing infringing mark use caused deception or confusion, determined by analyzing signs' appearance and sound and considering surrounding circumstances; actual confusion need not be proven, though beneficial. The High Court determined confusion/deception by examining party business activity similarities; the CoA differed, holding the inquiry should concern whether infringing mark use falls within registered trademark specification coverage, and that "words/phrases in the specification of goods and services should be given its natural and ordinary meaning within the context of which they are used."

**Computer-Generated Documentary Evidence Admission:** Plaintiffs submitted two emails showing actual public confusion; email makers weren't called as witnesses, and the High Court found the emails dubious and discarded them. The CoA disagreed, citing Section 90(A) of the Evidence Act 1950, holding that a "document produced in the course of a computers ordinary use may be admitted in evidence without the need to call the maker." Based on case facts, requirements were rightfully satisfied and the High Court should have admitted the emails.

**Passing Off:** Establishing tort of passing off requires, per *Reckitt & Colman Products Ltd v Borden Inc.* [1990] 1 WLR 491: (a) plaintiff possesses goodwill or reputation attached to goods or services; (b) defendant misrepresents to the public, leading or likely leading them to believe defendant's goods/services are plaintiff's goods/services; and (c) plaintiff proves suffering damage from defendant's misrepresentation. The High Court found Plaintiffs possessed requisite goodwill/reputation but failed establishing that goodwill as well-known, thus failing passing off establishment. The CoA disagreed, holding "there were not such requirements in law that the Plaintiffs' goodwill must be well-known" — Plaintiffs need only show business identifiers are "distinctive to Plaintiffs." Though Plaintiffs and Defendants conducted non-common businesses, both engaged in allied field business, so infringing mark use by Defendants would naturally constitute misrepresentation. The CoA held "the law imposes an obligation on business newcomers to ensure no confusion would arise from use of infringing marks or names." Regarding damages, the CoA agreed with the High Court that Plaintiffs suffered no damages; the CoA noted that "in an action for tort of passing off, damage is an essential element which the Plaintiffs must establish," though courts may infer damage likelihood where businesses directly compete — here the CoA didn't infer damages based on available evidence.

**Unlawful Interference with Trade:** The CoA agreed with Plaintiffs that "once a claim for trademark infringement and/or passing off is established, automatically the tort of unlawful interference with trade will also be made out."

### Malaysia: A Decision on Relevance and Cause of Action Relating to Property Managers

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-a-decision-on-relevance-and-cause-of-action-relating-to-property-managers/
**Date:** No publication date shown.

Appraisal Property Management Sdn Bhd ("APM"), JLL Property Services (Malaysia) Sdn Bhd ("JLLP") and Jones Lang Wootton Ltd ("JLWL"), (collectively "the Appellants") v Singham Sulaiman Sdn Bhd ("the Respondent"), is an appeal concerning two consolidated suits from the High Court, based on the tort of passing off and removal of registered trade mark from the Register.

The Appellants and the Respondent were companies involved in the real estate business. In the first suit, the Respondent claimed that APM and JLLP committed the tort of passing off by representing their real estate services as those of the Respondent's, under the names of "Jones Lang LaSalle" and/or "JLL", while JLLP counterclaimed against the Respondent based on the tort of passing off, namely that the Respondent had passed off its real estate services as being associated with the services offered by the Jones Lang LaSalle group of companies (JLL Group).

In the second suit, JLWL applied to the High Court under Section 45(1)(a) of the Trade Marks Act 1976 ("TMA") to remove Jones Lang Wootton registered trademarks from the Register (used and registered by the Respondent), alleging it was wrongfully registered by Respondent contrary to the terms of a Deed of Sub-Licence of Name and a Deed of Covenant.

The High Court found in favour of the Respondent, dismissing JLLP's counterclaim and the second suit. Aggrieved, the Appellants appealed to the Court of Appeal ("the CoA"). The CoA allowed the appeal and found that the High Court had misled itself by focusing on irrelevant considerations, ordering a retrial without addressing the trademark issues, as it found the High Court's undue focus on collateral issues prejudiced a fair and proper consideration of principal issues in the case.

**Relevance of breach of Valuers, Appraisers and Estate Agents Act 1981 ("the VAEA 1981"):** The High Court found that APM and JLLP breached Section 23 of the VAEA 1981, which requires the majority shareholding and operation of a company offering valuers/appraiser/estate agent services to be owned and controlled by registered valuers, estate agents and property managers. The alleged illegality related to breach of Section 23 of the VAEA 1981, whereas the cause of action was based on tort of passing off and trademark; the CoA, referring to the Federal Court in *Asia Television Ltd & Anor v Viwa Video Sdn Bhd & Connected Cases [1984] 2 MLJ 304*, held that in absence of a nexus between the alleged illegality and the cause of action, the Appellants' rights in the tort of passing off would not be defeated. Further, the VAEA 1981 provides for its own penalties for any breach of its provisions; it is improper for a Court to impose an "additional penalty" not stated in the Act by disqualifying an alleged defaulter from defending his/her intellectual property rights. The issue of illegality was only raised during cross examination of Appellants' witness after close of the Respondent's case and was never pleaded; parties are bound by their own pleadings, so the High Court erred in law by addressing the unpleaded issue of illegality.

**Application of Section 165 of the Evidence Act 1950 ("EA"):** The CoA found the High Court had usurped its power under Section 165 of the EA 1950 in ordering JLLP to produce documents to prove the illegality. Section 165 should be invoked only with the objective of discovering relevant facts or obtaining proper proof of such facts. Owing to the lack of pleading and absence of nexus between the illegality and the claim, illegality was an irrelevant point and the CoA ought not to have invoked Section 165 without due regard to the fundamental question of relevance.

**Lifting the corporate veil:** The Respondent had pleaded that the corporate veil should be pierced to meet the Appellants' case that the JLW Marks are not distinctive of the plaintiff. The High Court instead lifted the corporate veil to decide on the alleged breach of VAEA. It is settled law that pleadings on piercing the corporate veil must be precise on the ground on which it is sought; courts are not allowed to pierce the veil for any non-pleaded, ancillary purpose, nor purely on ground of interest of justice — only in special circumstances involving actual fraud, fraud at common law, or equitable fraud.

The CoA decision reiterated the importance of courts not getting carried away by ancillary matters and focusing on relevant issues within the parameters of the pleadings, and reinforced that intellectual property rights accorded to one cannot be denied on the basis of non-adherence or breach of an unrelated statutory provision absent a nexus between the breach and the rights asserted.

### Malaysia: Trademark Infringement and Obligation of Online Platform Providers Defined by Court

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-trademark-infringement-and-obligation-of-online-platform-providers-defined-by-court/
**Date:** Not shown

*A & M Beauty Wellness Sdn. Bhd.* ("the Plaintiff") v *Shopee Mobile Malaysia Sdn. Bhd.* ("the Defendant") concerns the obligations of e-commerce platform providers with reference to any counterfeit or trademark-infringing products offered for sale via their platform.

The Plaintiff is a company in the beauty and cosmetic industry, selling its product through a "Membership Business Model" whereby only members have access to purchase and sell the products. The mark "AM PROFESSIONAL SKIN CARE" ("the AM Mark"), which identifies the Plaintiff's product, has been registered under the Trade Mark Act 1976 ("TMA 1976").

The Defendant operates an online platform, www.shopee.com.my ("the Marketplace"), allowing third party sellers to list and sell goods. The Plaintiff alleged counterfeit products bearing the AM Mark were being offered for sale on the Marketplace, and emailed the Defendant requesting removal and/or the particulars of the sellers. Failing a favourable response, the Plaintiff applied to court for a mandatory interim interlocutory injunction for the Defendant to remove all listings similar to the Plaintiff's bearing the Plaintiff's trademark.

The IP Court in Kuala Lumpur dismissed the Plaintiff's application, applying the elements needed to grant an injunction: (1) a serious question to be tried; (2) balance of justice/convenience favours granting the injunction; (3) damages are not an adequate final remedy; (4) whether the Plaintiff gave a meaningful undertaking as to damages; and (5) if factors are evenly balanced, the status quo should be maintained.

**Serious question to be tried:** The Court found the Plaintiff had failed to adduce sufficient evidence that the Marketplace products were counterfeit. The Defendant provided a mechanism to remove infringing products (requiring a complete list of offending products and evidence of infringement), which the Plaintiff did not use. The Defendants were not sellers restrainable from selling a product but operators of a marketplace; requiring the Defendant to screen and remove every infringing product would undermine e-commerce operation, and the Defendant lacked the technical capability to pre-screen every listing or "autoblock" a particular product. Separately, the AM Mark was registered under one Chua Siok, not the Plaintiff; the Plaintiff claimed an assignment via deed, but the court held the assignment was not registered per the TMA 1976, and the Plaintiff (an unregistered assignee) lacked locus standi to sue. The Plaintiff's argument that the Defendant should prevent sellers from selling at a lower price was held unsubstantiated, as price control would restrict resellers' rights contrary to Section 4 of the Competition Act 2010, and any cause of action should lie against third-party sellers, not the Defendant.

**Balance of convenience:** Favoured the Defendant, since an injunction would impose a continual screening obligation, and the injunction sought was too wide and unclear.

**Damages adequate:** Damages were found adequate given the monetary nature of the claim.

**Plaintiff's undertaking as to damages inadequate:** Given the absence of adequate evidence of the Plaintiff's financial standing.

**Status Quo:** Since the Plaintiff's loss could be compensated by money, status quo would be maintained absent the injunction; conversely, wrongful grant of an injunction would not maintain the Defendant's status quo, as it could expose the Defendants to action from other legitimate users whose products might be wrongly removed.

The Court also held that for mandatory interim injunctions, applicants must additionally show an unusually strong and clear case with special circumstances, which were not met here. The decision relieved online platform providers of an obligation to act as a watchdog against trademark infringement on their platforms; the article notes it "remains to be seen if the decision will withstand the test of time."

### Malaysia: Traders Can Be Found Liable for the Tort of Passing-Off

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-traders-can-be-found-liable-for-the-tort-of-passing-off/
**Date:** Not shown

On 12 February 2019, the Malaysian Court of Appeal delivered a judgement in *Lifomax Woodbuild Sdn Bhd v Amsteel Mills Sdn Bhd* concerning the tort of passing-off.

Lifomax Woodbuild Sdn Bhd ("the Appellant") appealed a High Court decision dated 24 September 2013 that had ruled in favour of Amsteel Mills Sdn Bhd ("the Respondent"), a steel manufacturer of steel bars and wire rods. The Appellant trades in building materials including steel products for construction companies.

The Respondent had sued the Appellant for passing-off after the Appellant reportedly supplied deformed steel bars of inferior quality to a construction company, Mammoth Empire Construction Sdn. Bhd. ("Mammoth"), represented as originating from the Respondent, using fake Mill Test Certificates and Product Tags of the Respondent — leading Mammoth to complain to the Respondent.

The High Court ruled the Appellant had committed passing-off by misrepresenting the products as originating from the Respondent, misleading Mammoth and causing a likelihood of damage to the Respondent's goodwill and reputation; the claim was allowed.

On appeal, the Appellant argued: (1) it was merely a trader (goods purchased from NBH Marketing Sdn. Bhd. and on-sold), not the manufacturer, so the goods should not be considered the Appellant's goods for passing off purposes; and (2) the High Court judge failed to approach the testimony of witnesses PW2 and PW3 (from Mammoth) with caution, given concurrent proceedings between Mammoth and the Appellant in the Shah Alam High Court for goods sold and delivered.

The Respondent countered that although not manufactured by the Appellant, the goods were sold by the Appellant and hence it was liable, and that the High Court judge relied not only on PW2's evidence but also on DW1's admission, documentary evidence (Malaysian Standards, Purchase Order, test results, police photographs of product tags).

The Court of Appeal concurred with the Respondent, holding "the tort of passing off was indeed made out." The decision established that traders may be held liable for this tort by misrepresenting others' goods as their own, regardless of whether they manufactured the items — although NBH originally produced the steel, when the Appellant resold the materials to Mammoth, they became the Appellant's goods for purposes of the tort claim. The Court also found the High Court judge was aware of the Shah Alam proceedings, that the Appellant never challenged PW1/PW2 credibility during cross-examination, and that the outcome of the present case had no bearing on the separate goods-sold-and-delivered claim.

**Outcome:** The Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal with costs, and the High Court's decision was retained.

### Malaysia: Understanding the Value of Trademarks Co-Existence in Other Jurisdictions

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-understanding-the-value-of-trademarks-co-existence-in-other-jurisdictions/
**Date:** Not shown on the page.

In the High Court case of *Merck KGAA v Xtalic Corporation*, Merck KGAA filed an appeal against the Malaysian Registrar of Trade Marks' dismissal of opposition to a trademark registration application on 2 July 2018.

Merck KGAA, a German private limited company, operates in pharmaceutical, chemical, and life sciences sectors, plus electronics, printing, coatings, cosmetics, food, and biotechnology. The company owns the registered trademark "XIRALLIC" in classes 1, 2, 37 and 42 in Malaysia, with first use dating to August 2002 domestically and 2000 internationally. The mark is registered across numerous countries including Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Colombia, Germany, Ecuador, the European Community, Greece, Hong Kong, Indonesia, Ireland, Israel, India, Japan, Morocco, Mexico, New Zealand, Peru, Philippines, Pakistan, Singapore, South Korea, South Africa, Thailand, Tunisia, Turkey, Taiwan, the United States and Venezuela.

Xtalic Corporation, incorporated in Massachusetts, specializes in proprietary metal alloy technologies for coating applications. On 20 July 2009, Xtalic filed a trademark application for "XTALIC" in class 2 with Malaysia's Intellectual Property Corporation. The company has registrations in Brazil, Taiwan, and the US, plus international Madrid Convention registrations designating China, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, South Korea, and Spain.

Upon discovering Xtalic's application, Merck filed an opposition notice. The Registrar dismissed this opposition on 03 May 2018, prompting Merck's High Court appeal on five grounds, contending the Registrar erred regarding first user relevance, mark distinguishability, goods differences, independent conception, and co-existence in other jurisdictions.

The High Court addressed each ground systematically. Regarding first use, the court found "the Registrar had not erred in her finding that there is no likelihood of confusion or deception between the Plaintiff's mark and the Defendant's mark," determining confusion assessments should rely on probable effects of normal and fair use rather than notional fair use, citing precedents from Singapore, the European Commission, and Malaysian trademark jurisprudence.

The marks were deemed sufficiently different aesthetically and functionally. While both registered under Class 2 (coatings), the court found Merck's goods focused on aesthetic applications whereas Xtalic's emphasized engineering functionality: "The goods of the Plaintiff and the Defendant are different in nature for different purposes" and operate through "different trade channels," making confusion unlikely despite coexistence.

Regarding co-existence in other jurisdictions, the High Court affirmed this as "an indication that there is no likelihood of deception or confusion between the parties' respective marks." The Korean Intellectual Property Office had similarly dismissed Merck's opposition, finding the marks "quite distinguishable and not similar in phonetic sound."

**Outcome:** The High Court disallowed the appeal, retaining the Registrar's decision with costs awarded.

### Malaysia: Effect of Disclaimers in the Scope of Trademark

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-effect-of-disclaimers-in-the-scope-of-trademark/
**Date:** Not shown on page

On 17 July 2019, the Court of Appeal delivered a judgement in *Nor Yanni bt Adom & Anor v Ortus Expert White* [2020] 1 MLJ 101 concerning breach of dealership agreement, trademark infringement and passing-off.

Ortus Expert White ("the Respondent") is the owner of a registered trademark "Royal Expert White" featuring a crown device. Ms. Nor Yanni Binti Adom ("the 1st Appellant") entered a Dealership Agreement dated 9 September 2015 with the Respondent. Mr. Noorfaezal Bin Romli ("the 2nd Appellant") is the sole proprietor of Rafica Resources and distributed the Respondent's products alongside the 1st Appellant, including a skin whitening cream bearing the Respondent's registered trademark. The 1st and 2nd Appellants also sold a whitening cream bearing the mark "Real Expert White" with a diamond-shaped device.

The Respondent sued, alleging the 1st Appellant breached the dealership agreement and both Appellants infringed the Respondent's trademark and passed off the Respondent's trademark. The High Court ruled the 1st and 2nd Appellants liable on all three causes of action.

The Appellants appealed to the Court of Appeal, relying on a disclaimer attached to the registration providing no exclusive right to the words "Royal" and "Expert White". The Court of Appeal found the High Court's comparison of essential features flawed, holding "the only striking similarities between the two marks are the disclaimed words 'Expert White'" — as disclaimed words are not protected under the trademark registration, the two marks are distinct, not likely to cause confusion, and the Appellants did not infringe. The Court of Appeal also held the Respondent did not fully discharge its burden of proof regarding trademark infringement and passing off.

On passing off, the Appellants argued the product "Royal Expert Whitening Cream" was registered under a non-party, Ortus Expert Cosmetics Sdn Bhd, not the Respondent — a point the High Court judge failed to notice, as the CDCR list of registered products contained products of both the Respondent and the non-party. The High Court judge had found the Respondent enjoyed goodwill in its business related to the Respondent's mark and get-up, and that the Respondent proved loss caused by the Appellants' misrepresentation.

Contrary to this, the Court of Appeal considered a press statement issued by the Ministry of Health (MOH) that banned the product "Royal Expert Whitening Cream" for containing mercury, warning sellers/distributors to stop sale and distribution and warning the public to seek medical advice for adverse reactions. The Court of Appeal agreed with the Appellants that this destroyed the element of goodwill in the Respondent's "Royal Expert" branding and product, concluding the Respondent's loss was due to this matter, not the Appellants' misrepresentation.

**Outcome:** The Court of Appeal ruled the element of misrepresentation failed as the Respondent could not establish goodwill, there was non-infringement of the Respondent's trademark, and consequently no breach of the dealership agreement. The Court unanimously allowed the appeal with costs, setting aside the High Court's decision.

### Malaysia: Biscuits Battle in Court

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-biscuits-battle-in-court/
**Date:** Not shown

In the High Court of Malaya case of *Munchy Food Industries Sdn Bhd v Huasin Food Industries Sdn Bhd*, Munchy Food Industries Sdn Bhd ("the Plaintiff") brought an action against Huasin Food Industries Sdn Bhd ("the Defendant") for trademark infringement and passing off.

The Plaintiff owns the LEXUS Mark, registered on 23 Jan 1998 and used on biscuit packaging since then. The Defendant applied to register the LEX Mark on 25 September 2015; the Plaintiff opposed. The Plaintiff alleged infringement and passing off in respect of its mark and get-up. The Defendant counterclaimed for expungement of the Plaintiff's mark, alleging a likelihood of deception/confusion with Toyota's LEXUS Mark and that the Plaintiff's mark was not distinctive over its biscuits.

Key issues for the High Court: (1) whether the Defendant, as an alleged infringer, is an aggrieved party entitled to file for expungement; (2) whether the prohibition of likelihood of deception/confusion applies to the first bona fide user (1st user); (3) whether the Defendant can rely on a stranger's trademark (Toyota's LEXUS) to expunge the Plaintiff's mark; (4) whether internet searches such as Wikipedia can be relied on as evidence; and (5) whether there is any effect on distinctiveness when a house brand and sub-brand are used together.

The Court dismissed the Plaintiff's submission that an infringer is disentitled from applying for expungement, relying on the Federal Court decision in *LB (Lian Bee) Confectionary Sdn Bhd v QAF Ltd*, holding the Defendant satisfied the meaning of an aggrieved party under Section 45(1)(a) of the Trade Marks Act (TMA).

The Court found the Plaintiff was the 1st user of its mark in Malaysia, and under Section 14(1)(a) TMA, the likelihood of deception/confusion prohibition does not apply to a 1st user — only a second or subsequent user may cause the likelihood of confusion. Accordingly, the Court dismissed the Defendant's counterclaim to remove the Plaintiff's mark.

The Court held the Defendant may rely on a stranger's trademark (Toyota's LEXUS) as a ground in the expungement application, as the TMA does not prevent such consideration; however, the Defendant failed to discharge the burden of proving a likelihood of deception/confusion between the Plaintiff's mark and Toyota's LEXUS mark, so expungement did not proceed on that ground.

On internet/Wikipedia evidence, the Court found such search results doubtful due to lack of information about authorship and source, ruling that internet and Wikipedia search results are not considered in trademark and passing-off cases.

On house brand/sub-brand distinctiveness, the Court held the Defendant failed to prove the Plaintiff's mark was not distinctive; house brand and sub-brand are, in law, "stand alone" trademarks with their own distinctiveness when used together, provided they meet the distinctiveness criteria under Section 37(c) TMA.

On passing off, the Plaintiff proved goodwill in its mark and biscuit get-up; despite differences, the Court found striking similarities between the get-ups, concluding the Defendant misrepresented its biscuits as the Plaintiff's and proved a likelihood of damage to the Plaintiff's goodwill. The Plaintiff's claim was allowed with costs; the Defendant's counterclaim was dismissed.

Huasin Food Industries Sdn Bhd ("the Appellant") appealed to the Court of Appeal, arguing the High Court (i) failed to properly assess likelihood of deception/confusion for infringement; (ii) erred on misrepresentation for passing-off; and (iii) failed to compare the whole packaging of the Appellant's Trademark against Munchy Food Industries Sdn Bhd's ("the Respondent") Trademark and their circumstances of use.

The Court of Appeal held that both parties are genuine traders and the TMA is not meant to give any form of monopoly to traders affecting consumers — the TMA provides for genuine traders who claim probable confusion to seek suitable orders from the registrar to vary the mark itself. It also held the TMA is framed such that the Court should consider whether parties should have sought orders for co-existence of the marks to avoid confusion and generate healthy competition for public benefit, as opposed to exercising minute technical arguments relying on cases from jurisdictions where "co-existence" may not be part of the law.

### Malaysia: JLLP Appeal's Allowed

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-jllp-appeals-allowed/
**Date:** No date shown on the page.

In March 2019, the Court of Appeal delivered a judgment in *Appraisal Property Management Sdn. Bhd.; JLL Property Services (Malaysia) Sdn. Bhd; & Jones Lang Wootton Ltd* ("JLLP") v *Singham Sulaiman Sdn. Bhd* ("SSSB"), in which JLLP appealed against the High Court's decision in the 1st suit (which ruled against JLLP on SSSB's claim for the tort of passing off of real estate services and dismissed JLLP's counterclaim) and the 2nd suit (expunging JLLP's application to remove Jones Lang Wootton Registered Trademarks from the Register).

In the 1st Suit, SSSB claimed JLLP committed passing off of real estate services under the names "Jones Lang LaSalle" and/or "JLL", while JLLP counterclaimed that SSSB passed off its services as associated with the JLL Group. Evidence showed SSSB uses several trademarks in its real estate business: (1) "Jones Lang Wootton"; (2) a Jones Lang Wootton composite mark for services under Class 36; (3) "Jones Lang"; and (4) "JLW" mark.

In the 2nd Suit, JLLP applied to remove trademark (2) above, claiming it was wrongfully registered by SSSB contrary to the terms of a Deed of Sub-Licence dated 6 July 1993 and Deed of Covenant dated 1 September 1992 signed by SSSB.

After a full trial, the High Court allowed SSSB's claim in part and dismissed JLLP's counterclaim and the 2nd suit. JLLP appealed.

Background: under the Deed of Sub-Licence between JLW Pacific Ltd. and SSSB, SSSB was enabled to continue using the Jones Lang Wootton name and JLW mark. Following a series of assignments, London proprietors assigned all IP rights, including trademarks and goodwill, to JLWL. In 2002, SSSB entered a "Global Operating Framework" (GOF); in 2006, SSSB applied to register Jones Lang Wootton Registered Trademark (2), which was allowed by the Registrar. GOF was terminated in 2012. The High Court considered whether SSSB committed passing off by associating itself with Jones Lang after GOF's termination, and whether SSSB's registered trademarks ought to be expunged. The High Court ruled in favour of SSSB, as the Defendants were unable to adduce evidence in the 1st suit, finding SSSB had not misrepresented its business as associated with JLL Group services and that JLLP's counterclaim was an afterthought filed to stifle SSSB's claim. The 2nd Suit was dismissed because JLLP was not aggrieved by the Registered Trademarks and the trademarks were not registered by way of fraud on the Registrar.

On appeal, the Court of Appeal found that Defendants' counsel had focused the Court only on the issue of alleged illegality and collateral matters (lifting the corporate veil, loan documents, etc.), causing the High Court to lose focus on the relevant issues of passing off, counter-passing off, and expungement of the Plaintiff's registration of the JLW Marks. The Court of Appeal allowed the appeal and remitted the case for retrial, unanimously deciding "the appeal must be allowed with cost and the High Court's decision to be set aside," followed by a retrial before another judge on the alleged trademark infringement and expungement of SSSB's registered trademarks.

### Malaysia – Passing Off Much?

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-passing-off-much/
**Date:** Not shown

This is the case between Guangzhou Light Industry & Trade Group Ltd ("the 1st Plaintiff"), Guangzhou Eaglecoin Enterprises Group Corporation (also known as Guangdong Cannery) ("the 2nd Plaintiff") and Kim Guan Hap Kee Sdn Bhd ("the 3rd Plaintiff") and Lintas Superstore Sdn Bhd ("the Defendant").

The main issues discussed are that canned food, specifically fried dace with salted black beans and unsalted beans, bearing the mark "Eagle Coin" are sold in the Defendant's supermarket located in Kota Kinabalu, Borneo. Plaintiffs filed suit against the Defendant, claiming trademark infringement and passing-off.

Under the tort of passing-off, three essential elements are considered: (1) that the Plaintiffs have goodwill attached to the business regarding the mark used in respect of the Plaintiffs' goods or services; (2) that the Defendant has misrepresented to the public by using a mark likely to confuse or deceive the public into believing the Defendant's goods or services are the Plaintiffs'; and (3) that there is a likelihood of damage to the Plaintiffs due to the Defendant's misrepresentation.

The 3rd Plaintiff is the sole registered user of the "Eagle Coin" trademark in Malaysia and has marketed fried dace under this mark for some time; the Defendant had previously purchased the 3rd Plaintiff's goods and sold them in its supermarket. The 3rd Plaintiff established goodwill attached to the fried dace marketed in Malaysia under "Eagle Coin", and established at trial that the good sold by the Defendant, despite common origin, was not the same good sold by the 3rd Plaintiff, as the Defendant's good did not comply with requirements of Malaysian law (including the "halal" requirement, which the 3rd Plaintiff's good complied with but the Defendant's product did not). The reputation of the 3rd Plaintiff would be damaged if Malaysian customers confused the source of the Defendant's product; the Plaintiffs succeeded in establishing the claim under passing-off.

Non-arguable facts considered: (1) the 1st Plaintiff was the registered owner of the "Eagle Coin" trademark in Malaysia; (2) the 2nd Plaintiff is authorised to market canned fried dace with the trademark; (3) the 3rd Plaintiff is the sole registered user of the trademark in Malaysia; and (4) the Defendant is neither the registered owner nor registered user of the trademark in Malaysia. The Defendant did not disagree that no permission was obtained from the 3rd Plaintiff for marketing the product in Malaysia; the only evidence tendered regarding consent from the 1st/2nd Plaintiff was that the Defendant purchased the goods (or caused them to be purchased) from one of the 2nd Plaintiff's outlets in China. The Judge found the trademark rights of all the Plaintiffs had been infringed by the Defendant.

**Outcome:** The Judge found the Plaintiffs succeeded in proving trademark infringement and passing-off against the Defendant and ordered the Defendant to pay costs of MYR30,000.00 to the Plaintiffs.

### ASEAN: Trade Mark Intensive Industries and Their Economic Contribution

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/asean-trade-mark-intensive-industries-and-their-economic-contribution/
**Date:** Not shown

A study was conducted by Frontier Economics Ltd. together with the International Trademark Association to assess the economic contribution of trade mark intensive industries in Malaysia, Indonesia, Philippines, Thailand, and Singapore. Trade mark intensive industries are defined as those industries that have an above average use of trade marks per employee. The economic contribution of the five economies was assessed by economic consensus, survey data of business activities, and national accounts data prepared by statistics authorities. The direct contributions taken into account included terms of employment and the business output generated by trade mark intensive industries. An economic methodology was used to estimate whether value added per employee changes when an employee moves from a non-trade mark intensive industry to a trade mark intensive industry.

In Malaysia, the direct contribution of trade mark intensive industries was measured at 30.3% of the national GDP. The majority of the contribution was from the manufacturing sector, which on its own accounts for 17% of national GDP, followed by wholesale and retail, and information and communication sector as next largest contributors with just over 5% of national GDP. An analysis of remuneration data shows about 12% was paid to workers by trade mark intensive industries. Further analysis of the manufacturing industry shows that computer, electronics and optical products, and coke and refined petroleum products dominate; these are also significant export industries — fuel accounts for about 20% of exports by value while electrical and office machinery accounts for another 34%. Food and chemical products, while less significant, are nevertheless strongly trade mark intensive; other manufacturing industries such as agriculture, mining and quarrying activities may not be trade mark intensive in and of themselves but have a linkage to trade mark intensive industries.

In comparison across countries in the study, direct contribution of trade mark intensive industries to GDP was just under a fifth for the Philippines and just under a third for Malaysia, compared to about 36% as measured in the EU. Contribution to GDP rises when indirect effects via output-linkages are taken into account, ranging from a low of 28% in the Philippines to a high of 60% in Malaysia — attributable mainly to primary industries such as agriculture, natural resources and extractive industries, significantly so in Malaysia given its significant primary sector; the Philippines is an outlier with its agricultural sector showing low dependence on trade mark intensive industries.

Trade mark intensive industries' contribution to employment in the study countries accounts for about 38% to 50%. Remuneration in trade mark intensive industries is about 12% to 30% higher compared to non-trademark intensive industries. Within manufacturing, computer and electronic-related products exhibited significant economic contribution, especially in the Philippines, Malaysia, Thailand, and Singapore, correlating with international trade developed over recent decades to meet cross-border supply chain demands. Beverages, and to a lesser extent food industries, were found to be trade mark intensive and economically significant in all study countries, explained by the importance of raw material availability and labour costs. The econometric analysis shows the economic payoffs of trade mark intensiveness across all five countries increases value added per worker by around 90%.

The study exhibited the importance of trade mark intensive industries in the five countries and potential effects on performance metrics such as exports and value added per employee, highlighting the connection between trade mark intensive industries, export, and productivity and their implications for national economic growth — though it does not conclude that trade mark protection will inevitably boost economic growth, given dependence on factors such as national policies on exportation and trading.

### Malaysia: Estoppel and Breach of Contract

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-estoppel-and-breach-of-contract/
**Date:** Not shown

In the Court of Appeal case of *Mohamad bin S Ahmad & Ors v Lembaga Pengelola Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka* [2019] 9 MLJ 315, all partners of Darul Fikir — Mohamad Bin S. Ahmad, Sheikh Ahmad Bin Mohamad, Sheikh Mohammad Ayman Bin Mohamad and Sheikh Mohammad Syaamil Bin Mohamad ("the Appellants") — appealed a High Court decision dated 7 August 2018 that dismissed the Appellants' claim for an injunction to restrain Lembaga Pengelola Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka ("the Respondent") from issuing an open tender to invite a third party to bid for a contract to publish and supply a book entitled "Mushaf Al-Quran Bertajwid dan Berwarna" ("the Book") without the Appellants' written consent.

The Appellants are the exclusive distributors and copyright owners of the Book. The Appellants brought an action for breach of a contract dated 2 September 2016 when the Respondent issued the tender without the Appellants' written consent. The High Court ruled the Respondent did not breach the contract, as the contract was for a one-year duration which had lapsed and a supplemental contract had not been signed; the contract had been duly performed and the Respondent had paid the Appellants in full, so there was no provision preventing the Respondent from issuing the tender.

On appeal, the issues were whether the Respondent could issue the tender without the Appellants' written consent after the contract expired, and whether the Respondent had expressly acknowledged that copyright in the colour code of the Book was conferred on the Appellants.

The Appellants' counsel emphasized the claim was entirely on breach of contract, not copyright infringement, and that both parties agreed the Appellants are the copyright owners of the Book. The Respondent's counsel argued the contract's obligations were no longer enforceable as it had expired.

The Court of Appeal referred to relevant provisions: Recital (1) — Dr. Taha is the owner of the copyright in the colour code of the Book; Clause 13.1 — the copyright in the colour code of the Book is owned by the Appellants because Darul Fikir is the exclusive distributor of the Book granted by the original copyright owner, subject to payment of royalty/honorarium; Clause 15.3 — allows the Respondent at all times to appoint a third party to manage publication, printing and supply of the Book provided prior written consent of the Appellants or the copyright owner is obtained.

The Court of Appeal held "there is an error of law in the approach of the High Court that no copyright of the Book is conferred on the Appellants" per Clauses 13.1 and 14.1, and noted the Respondent had initially agreed in Clause 13.1 that the copyright was owned by the Appellants but was now denying this.

The Court of Appeal held that statements in Clauses 13.1 and 15.1 of the contract are statements of fact which the parties are bound by estoppel from denying, even though the contract had expired; it would be inequitable, based on the principle of estoppel, for the Respondent to allow a third party to publish and supply the Book without the Appellants' written consent even though the contract had expired.

**Outcome:** The Court unanimously decided the appeal was allowed with costs and the High Court's decision was set aside.

### Copyright Article – Telekung (Malaysia: Copyright Dispute on Telekung)

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/copyright-article-telekung/
**Date:** No publication date is shown on this page.

In the case of *Siti Khadijah Apparel Sdn Bhd* ("the Plaintiff") v *Ariani Textiles & Manufacturing (M) Sdn Bhd* ("the Defendant"), the High Court of Malaya decided on whether copyright subsists in "telekung", an outfit usually worn by Muslim women during prayers.

Four issues were decided by the Court: (1) does a telekung constitute a "graphic work" or a "work of artistic craftsmanship" within the meaning of Section 3 of the Copyright Act 1987?; (2) if a telekung constitutes a "graphic work," (a) whether a telekung is eligible for copyright given its religious function (interpreting Sections 7(2A) and (3)(a) of the Copyright Act) and (b) does the Plaintiff own copyright in the Plaintiff's telekung?; (3) if the Plaintiff owns the copyright ("Plaintiff's Copyright"), whether it has ceased under the then-applicable Section 7(6) of the Copyright Act (since repealed by the Copyright (Amendment) Act 2012, Act A1402); and (4) if the Plaintiff's Copyright has not ceased under s 7(6), has the Defendant infringed it under the first limb of s 36(1), and can the Defendant raise as a defence that it was unaware of the Plaintiff's Copyright and infringement?

**Issue 1:** The Court held that two-dimensional drawings regarding the design of the Plaintiff's telekung constitute a "graphic work" under Section 3(a) of the Copyright Act, and the telekung itself constitutes an "artistic work" under Section 3(a). However, the Plaintiff did not adduce evidence that the telekung is a "work of artistic craftsmanship" under Section 3(c).

**Issue 2:** The Court held Section 7(2A), which excludes copyright protection for any idea, procedure, method of operation or mathematical concept, does not bar copyright protection of a telekung on the ground of pure functionality; in any event, the Plaintiff's telekung is not purely functional, as it not only enables prayer but also gives comfort and elegance to the user. The Court held the Plaintiff owns copyright in its telekung, having fulfilled all conditions under the Act: sufficient effort was expended by the Plaintiff's designer to make it original in character; it had been reduced to "material form"; it was made by the Plaintiff's employee designer, a Malaysian citizen and "qualified person" under Section 3(a); it was first "published" in Malaysia under Section 4(1)(a) because first sold in Malaysia; it was made in Malaysia; and the design had not been registered as an industrial design under the Industrial Designs Act. This evidence was not rebutted by the Defendant.

**Issue 3:** Former Section 7(6) of the Copyright Act provided that copyright in a design capable of being registered as an industrial design, but not so registered, ceases once an article to which the design has been applied has been reproduced more than 50 times by an industrial process by the owner or licensee. The Court held the Plaintiff's Copyright had not ceased, because the telekung does not constitute an "industrial design" under Section 3 of the Industrial Designs Act, and is not applied to an "article" "by any industrial process or means" as defined in that Act.

**Issue 4:** The Court found the Defendant had infringed the Plaintiff's Copyright, having reproduced and distributed the Plaintiff's telekung, which is "an act the doing of which is controlled by copyright" under Section 36(1). There was sufficient objective similarity between the Plaintiff's and Defendant's telekung; the Defendant did not adduce credible evidence that its telekung was created without copying the Plaintiff's, and it was sold in the market subsequent to the Plaintiff's telekung. The Court found the Defendant's telekung copied a substantial part of the Plaintiff's, applying Lord Scott's decision in *Designers Guild* (the objective similarity shows direct copying of an identifiable part constituting a substantial part) and, even absent an identifiable part, "altered copying" under "Laddie's Test" (the Defendant incorporated a substantial part of the Plaintiff employee designer's independent skill and labour).

The Court further held the Defendant cannot claim as a defence that it was unaware of the Plaintiff's Copyright, had no knowledge of infringement, or did not intend to infringe, applying the Court of Appeal case of *Elster Metering Ltd & Anor v Damini Corporation Sdn Bhd & Anor [2012] 1 LNS 959* — these are not defences to copyright infringement, though the Defendant's lack of knowledge is a relevant factor for the Court in deciding whether to award damages and their quantum.

### Malaysia: Famous Filmmaker & Co. Continue the Fight

### Malaysia: A Copyright Saga

**Source:** https://adipven.com/case/malaysia-a-copyright-saga/
**Date:** No date shown

Chuah Aik King ("the Plaintiff") filed a suit against Keydonesoft Sdn. Bhd. ("the Defendant") at the High Court of Malaya at Kuala Lumpur, claiming that he owns copyright in two computer programmes: (1) "B3 Café Internet Café Billing Management with MyCard Solution Software" ("the B3 Programme") and (2) "Coin River Net Café Management System" ("the Coin River Programme"). The Plaintiff claimed the Defendant's software, "Keybilling Management System" ("the Keybilling Program"), infringed his copyright to both programmes.

The Plaintiff is the sole proprietor of "B Three Technology" and provided evidence that he created the "MyCard Solution" software ("the MyCard Solution Programme"), though the main engine to run the programme was created by ZYS, a company based in China. The Plaintiff claims he "created" the MyCard Solution Programme by advising and giving his ideas, then commissioning ZYS to create the programme, relying on a Copyright Agreement between him and ZYS dated 13 January 2009. The Plaintiff provided source codes to the two programmes, authored by one Mr. Feng Jiang ("Mr. Feng") from ZYS, China. The Plaintiff added that he sold the B3 programme to many entities except the Defendant, and alleged the Defendant's infringement led to a raid of the Defendant's premises, causing him to suffer a loss amounting to RM1,100,000 / approximately USD27,6073.00 [figure as stated in source; UNCERTAIN: the USD figure "27,6073.00" as extracted appears to contain a formatting irregularity/typo in the source and is recorded here exactly as presented].

The Defendant appointed ZYS to provide customised software for cybercafé operators; the Keybilling Programme was the software customized by ZYS, with copyright vesting in ZYS. Differences were pointed out between the graphic user interface of the B3 programme and the Keybilling programme. The Defendant testified that the Selangor State Government had required all cybercafé operators in Selangor to install the B3 programme, and that the Plaintiff was the sole distributor of the software, giving him leverage to impose an exorbitant fee.

The Court held that the Plaintiff cannot claim copyright in any function of a computer programme, as under Section 7(2A) of the Copyright Act, a function of a computer programme constitutes an idea and is not entitled to copyright protection; the Plaintiff therefore could not rely on the functions of the two programmes to support his suit. The legal burden was on the Plaintiff to adduce oral and documentary evidence proving copyright subsists in the two programmes and belongs to him, which the Court found he failed to do on several grounds: the Plaintiff testified that Mr. Feng authored the source codes of both programmes, but provided no evidence regarding Mr. Feng's qualification, training, experience or achievements in software creation and development; the Court further found the Plaintiff caused an adverse inference by not calling Mr. Feng to testify, and that the Plaintiff was not a credible witness. Under Section 37(1) of the Copyright Act, only a copyright owner may file suit for copyright infringement.

**Outcome:** The Court found the Plaintiff failed to discharge the legal and evidential burden to prove copyright subsists in the two programmes and that he is the owner, ruling that the Plaintiff had no right to institute the suit for copyright infringement, and dismissed the suit with costs.
